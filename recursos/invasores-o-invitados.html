Invaders or Invitees?

Roman Agency and the Movement of Foreign Peoples, ‘Barbarian’ Armies and foederati into and within the Roman Empire

← Papers & resourcesWorking version: 0.2.0 · Research editor: Hervin Fernández Aceves · Affiliation: El Colegio de Jalisco

When the Empire opened its frontiers

The movement of foreign peoples and armies into Roman territory is frequently narrated in terms of invasion. Yet Roman emperors, magistrates and commanders also authorised entry, recruited foreign troops, negotiated settlements and commissioned interventions in imperial conflicts. This chronology investigates those decisions without assuming that all foreign arrivals were either peaceful or invited.

The 20 selected records constitute a source-critical preview rather than a comprehensive census. An ancient allegation of invitation is not equivalent to an established act of Roman government.

Evidence, categories and historiography

Each entry identifies the Roman actor, the foreign party, the location, the mechanism of entry or intervention and an ancient testimony. The labels distinguish an explicitly attested arrangement, a contested interpretation and an unproven allegation. They do not measure the reliability of every detail in the narrative.

Military service, a foedus, land allocation and the continuing presence of foreign armies were not interchangeable arrangements. Nor should a usurper's request, a local urban agreement and an emperor's authorisation be treated as equivalent exercises of authority. The chronology brings these situations into comparison while preserving their differences.

Peter Brown’s account of Late Antiquity places institutional change, social relations and the reconfiguration of Roman society within a history of transformation, not simply political collapse. His work provides the period’s interpretative context, while the specific claims about invitations, armed groups and settlement must still be examined against the military and documentary evidence.

The works below serve distinct interpretative purposes; they do not all document particular invitations. These links identify pertinent scholarship, not a claim to have read every page of each volume.

Brown frames Late Antiquity as a period of social, religious and institutional transformation, with particular attention to authority and wealth. His work contextualises the period but does not itself corroborate a particular Roman summons to a foreign army.

Goffart puts forward a fiscal interpretation of accommodation; Halsall considers Roman political agency and migration mechanisms; Heather reconstructs Gothic group formation and interaction with Roman authorities. Their approaches offer competing questions for the settlement and admission cases.
